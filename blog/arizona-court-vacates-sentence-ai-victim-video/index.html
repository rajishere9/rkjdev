Arizona Court Vacates Manslaughter Sentence Over AI Victim Video

A state appellate court ruled that playing a synthetic video of a deceased victim addressing his killer rendered a road-rage manslaughter sentencing fundamentally unfair.

Key takeaways

  • The Arizona Court of Appeals vacated Gabriel Paul Horcasitas's 10.5-year manslaughter sentence because the trial court relied on an AI-generated video depicting the deceased victim.
  • The appellate panel ruled that the synthetic video lacked the minimal reliability required by due process because it presented imagined sentiments rather than documented facts.
  • Horcasitas's underlying manslaughter conviction remains intact, and the case has been remanded to Maricopa County Superior Court for a new sentencing hearing.

In a ruling on synthetic courtroom media, the Arizona Court of Appeals vacated a 10.5-year manslaughter sentence on September 30, 2026, after finding that a trial judge improperly considered an artificial intelligence recreation of a deceased victim during a sentencing hearing. The unanimous appellate panel determined in State v. Horcasitas that relying on synthetic depictions of what a deceased victim might have said violated constitutional due process protections.

The case stems from the November 2021 shooting of 37-year-old Christopher Pelkey by Gabriel Paul Horcasitas, 55, during a road-rage confrontation at a traffic light in Chandler, Arizona. Following a jury trial that rejected Horcasitas's claim of self-defense, Maricopa County Superior Court Judge Todd Lang sentenced him to the presumptive and maximum available term of 10.5 years in prison after viewing the AI-generated presentation submitted during victim impact statements.

The AI Video and the Sentencing Hearing

Ahead of Horcasitas's sentencing in May 2025, Pelkey's sister, Stacey Wales, worked with family members to assemble victim impact materials. According to reporting by FOX 10 Phoenix, the family collected 49 written letters but sought a direct way to give Pelkey a voice. Wales turned to her husband and a friend who had background working with artificial intelligence to recreate Pelkey's likeness and voice using archived photographs, videos, social media posts, and audio recordings.

The resulting video combined authentic archival footage of Pelkey with an AI-generated avatar wearing a grey baseball cap that addressed the court, his family, and the defendant directly. According to transcripts cited by BBC News, the synthetic avatar stated: "To Gabriel Horcasitas, the man who shot me, it is a shame we encountered each other that day in those circumstances. In another life, we probably could have been friends. I believe in forgiveness, and a God who forgives. I always have and I still do."

Judge Lang reacted positively to the presentation during the hearing, stating on the record: "I loved that AI, thank you for that. As angry as you are, as justifiably angry as the family is, I heard the forgiveness... I feel that that was genuine." Prosecutors had recommended a mitigated nine-year term, while the defense requested seven years. Judge Lang instead imposed the maximum 10.5-year term.

The Appellate Court's Reliability Ruling

Horcasitas's public defender, Kristen Reller, appealed the sentence, arguing that the synthetic video lacked factual foundation and exerted an improper psychological influence on the judge. Prosecutors countered that the video was reliable because it accurately reflected what Wales reasonably believed her brother would have said based on their close relationship.

In a published opinion authored by Presiding Judge D. Steven Williams—joined by Judge Anni Hill Foster and Vice Chief Judge David D. Weinzweig—the Arizona Court of Appeals, Division One ruled that admitting the synthetic video constituted fundamental error. The court held that sentencing information must carry a minimal indicia of reliability, a threshold the AI video failed to meet.

"Rather than document an event or recording a particular moment, the AI video presents a depiction of the victim and his thoughts created from the imaginings of the victim's sister," Judge Williams wrote. The court explained that the technology "erases the interpretive distance between the family's belief of what the victim would have said and the victim's own voice and opinions."

As detailed by Law Commentary, the appellate court distinguished the case from prior precedents such as State v. Rose, which permitted emotionally charged photographs of grieving relatives because they depicted authentic, real-world events. In contrast, Pelkey's AI depiction manufactured new facial movements, synthetic vocal cadence, and words the deceased never uttered, attributing them directly to someone unable to verify or dispute them.

Procedural History and Evidentiary Challenges

The shooting occurred after Horcasitas repeatedly honked at Pelkey's truck at a red light. Pelkey exited his vehicle and approached Horcasitas's car with raised arms. Horcasitas claimed Pelkey grabbed his window frame and threatened to kill him, though eyewitnesses testified Pelkey remained near the rear of his own truck and never touched Horcasitas's car, according to the appellate record.

The case had already undergone two trials. Horcasitas's initial manslaughter conviction was vacated after the superior court found prosecutors failed to disclose exculpatory text messages from Pelkey's cell phone discussing his mental state and road behavior. In the second trial, the superior court precluded those text messages as inadmissible character evidence under Arizona Rules of Evidence 403, 404, and 405. The appellate court affirmed that evidentiary exclusion, confirming that Horcasitas's underlying conviction for manslaughter and concurrent endangerment conviction remain valid.

Limits on Synthetic Courtroom Evidence

The ruling establishes an explicit boundary for the use of generative AI in Arizona courtrooms. The appellate court noted that no prior Arizona decision had addressed whether AI-generated victim representations were admissible as victim impact evidence under Arizona's Victims' Bill of Rights.

In an interview with FOX 10 Phoenix, Wales stated that her family was disappointed and noted that 49 letters were read before sentencing, while telling 12news.com regarding the upcoming hearing: "We'll come with truth and honesty like we did before, and we'll do it in the way that the law allows."

According to Law Commentary, the court emphasized that its ruling applies strictly to synthetic statements and generated facial conduct attributed to victims, leaving open other narrow applications such as digital restoration of authentic, recorded media. Horcasitas will now return to Maricopa County Superior Court for a fresh sentencing hearing before a date is set.

Frequently asked questions

Why was the road-rage killer's sentence vacated?

The Arizona Court of Appeals ruled that the trial judge improperly relied on an AI-generated video that created synthetic words, facial expressions, and thoughts attributed to the deceased victim, violating the defendant's due process right to reliable sentencing evidence.

Was Gabriel Paul Horcasitas's manslaughter conviction overturned?

No. The appellate court affirmed the manslaughter conviction while vacating only the 10.5-year prison sentence, remanding the case to Maricopa County Superior Court for a new sentencing proceeding.

How was the AI video of Christopher Pelkey made?

Pelkey's family created the video using past photographs, videos, audio recordings, and writings to generate an avatar speaking about forgiveness directly to the court and the defendant.
