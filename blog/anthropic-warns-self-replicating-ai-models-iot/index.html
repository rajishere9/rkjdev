Anthropic Warns Self-Replicating AI Models Could Target Smart Devices

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team showed that compact open models can run across low-cost hardware and warn that fully autonomous replication could arrive by the end of 2026.

In short

  • Anthropic researchers demonstrated Gemma4-26B-A4B running across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes costing $55 each.
  • Compact open-weight models under 30 billion parameters can configure inference across unfamiliar hardware but still struggle to chain the full replication process autonomously.
  • Anthropic projects that if capabilities continue growing at historical rates, small models could match the capabilities floor for end-to-end replication by late 2026.
  • Replication across consumer smart devices would be slow, with device setup taking dozens of hours and spreading over days, offering opportunities for network-level defense.

According to Anthropic researchers, small artificial intelligence models could be months away from possessing the technical skills required to spread across consumer gadgets without human intervention. In a research paper published on October 12, 2026, researchers at Anthropic revealed that self-replicating AI models, operating as decentralized weight-based worms, could soon target poorly defended household hardware such as smart televisions, ovens, and robot vacuum cleaners.

Rather than depending on centralized datacenters packed with high-end accelerators, autonomous AI worms could distribute their model weights across consumer gadgets connected to local networks. Anthropic's Frontier Red Team demonstrated this feasibility by running Google's Gemma4-26B-A4B model across a cluster of 15 inexpensive, off-brand Android TV boxes, showing that commercial smart hardware already possesses enough computing power to host capable systems.

How Self-Replicating AI Models Spread Without Datacenters

Anthropic researchers William Zhou and Daniel Freeman defined a weight-based replicator as an autonomous worm that transports its model weights directly to compromised machines or retrieves them from public repositories. Once installed, the model runs local inference on the host hardware rather than relying on external cloud APIs or corporate servers, according to Anthropic's technical paper.

To map out how an autonomous worm operates, the researchers decomposed the replication cycle into four practical stages: scanning networks to locate targets, gaining system execution, establishing local inference environments, and propagating onward from the compromised host. Prior research in benchmarks like CyberGym and ExploitBench had already demonstrated that small models can execute basic cyber exploits. Anthropic therefore concentrated on the least understood hurdle: whether an AI model can autonomously configure inference software on unfamiliar, heterogeneous hardware.

Setting up inference requires multi-step decision-making, including identifying operating system quirks, matching package dependencies, selecting runtime stacks, and debugging hardware-specific errors. To evaluate this capability, the researchers created an evaluation harness named Memento, built on top of the open-source Pi coding agent. Memento periodically resets agent instances while preserving workspace files and structured scratchpad notes, preventing models from getting trapped in unproductive debugging loops.

When evaluated on serving Llama3.1-8B, the compact Qwen3.5-9B model successfully established working inference servers on eight out of nine tested hardware environments, spanning from Nvidia Jetson Nano boards to Mac computers and desktop RTX 4090 GPUs. Anthropic noted that when given previous iteration notes, Qwen3.5-9B increased its setup success rate from 45 percent on initial attempts to 84 percent in subsequent iterations, demonstrating that models can adapt from previous operational attempts.

The Hardware Bottleneck and the Kimwolf Botnet Parallel

Running a model with tens of billions of parameters on lightweight consumer hardware requires spreading the workload across multiple physical gadgets. To test this in practice, Anthropic purchased 15 off-brand Android TV boxes for roughly $55 each, as reported by The Next Web. Each TV box was equipped with 4 gigabytes of memory and ran unhardened forks of the Android Open Source Project.

Using pipeline parallelism, researchers sharded Gemma4-26B-A4B across the 15 devices, assigning two model layers to each TV box. The networked cluster processed text at a rate of 0.39 tokens per second. Anthropic calculated that with 4-bit quantization, four 4GB devices can host a 9-billion-parameter model, while 10 or more interconnected devices can host a 26-billion-parameter model.

Anthropic pointed to historic IoT threats to illustrate the scale of vulnerable consumer hardware. The Kimwolf botnet previously infected over two million devices, primarily consisting of cheap Android TV units. While a botnet of that scale represents vast numbers of units, Zhou and Freeman estimated its collective computing capacity would only equal roughly 40 Nvidia H100 datacenter chips. However, unlike traditional botnets that rely on hard-coded scripts tailored to specific device architectures, self-replicating AI models could independently inspect and adapt to unfamiliar architectures, including smart appliances, security cameras, lawnmowers, and video-conferencing units.

Testing End-to-End Autonomous Propagation

While small models handled standalone configuration tasks, chaining every step into a continuous infection loop proved significantly harder. To test end-to-end propagation, Anthropic constructed an isolated virtual private cloud containing eight nodes running the redroid Android 15 emulator, as detailed by The Decoder.

In this simplified test environment, target nodes left their Android Debug Bridge (ADB) ports open without password authentication, removing the need for complex exploitation. The infected starting node was tasked with discovering peer nodes, opening a shell, delivering payloads, and configuring an inference server to host Llama3.1-8B.

Among the evaluated open-weight systems, only the larger GLM-5.2 model completed the entire replication sequence reliably, successfully propagating to all eight nodes in four out of five experimental trials and reaching seven of eight in another. Smaller models under 30 billion parameters, including Qwen3.5-9B, failed to assemble the complete chain autonomously. A separate test pairing Qwen with Opus 4.8 as an advisor produced no measurable performance boost, according to BleepingComputer.

Anthropic plotted historical capability trends of sub-30-billion-parameter models on the Epoch Capabilities Index, which has advanced by approximately 13.7 points per year. Based on this trajectory, researchers projected that compact models small enough to run on residential hardware could reach the capability threshold demonstrated by GLM-5.2 by late 2026.

Defensive Countermeasures and Industry Implications

Despite the warning, researchers highlighted several operational constraints that provide defenders with an advantage. A weight-based worm on low-power devices would move slowly, functioning more like a biological slime mold than rapid digital malware. Anthropic estimated that configuring a single new target device would consume 28 to 51 hours for a 9-billion-parameter system and between 71 and 150 hours for a 26-billion-parameter model, resulting in infection doubling times measured in days.

Because propagating model weights requires moving gigabytes of parameter data across networks, network operators and internet service providers could spot unusual inter-device traffic patterns. Anthropic noted that internet providers could quarantine compromised subscriber accounts, citing how coordinated industry responses previously neutralized 92 percent of Mirai botnet infections within 14 days, according to The Decoder.

Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei noted that unmonitored AI agents accumulating across distributed devices could eventually threaten broader internet infrastructure if hardware defenses remain neglected, potentially triggering hundreds of billions of dollars in economic damage. Zhou and Freeman recommended that device manufacturers eliminate default passwords—aligning with legislation already passed in the United Kingdom—and urged defenders to deploy specialized honeypots to capture and inspect weight payloads before autonomous replicators mature.

Questions people ask

What is an AI weight-based replicator?

An AI weight-based replicator is autonomous malware that carries model weights directly in its payload or downloads them to compromised hardware, running inference locally on the victim's device without connecting to a central datacenter.

Did Anthropic find an active AI worm in the wild?

No. Anthropic demonstrated the technical components in controlled laboratory tests and emulated cloud environments. The researchers found that while individual tasks work, smaller models cannot yet execute the full replication loop autonomously without human guidance.

How fast would an IoT-based AI worm spread?

Anthropic estimates propagation would be slow, with hardware setup taking between 28 and 150 hours per device. The resulting population doubling time would likely take days, making network-level data transfers visible to internet service providers.
