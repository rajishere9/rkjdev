Vera C. Rubin Observatory Discovers Two Faint Milky Way Companions

Early test data from the Chilean facility uncovered an ancient ultra-faint dwarf galaxy candidate and an accreted globular cluster hiding behind cosmic dust.

In short

  • Researchers identified Aquarius IV and Rubin-GC1 using Early Data Preview 2 (EDP2) from the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile.
  • Aquarius IV is an ancient, ultra-faint dwarf galaxy candidate situated roughly 355,000 light-years away in the outer Galactic halo.
  • Rubin-GC1 is a compact, intermediate-age globular cluster located behind thick cosmic dust and associated with the Sagittarius dwarf tidal stream.
  • The detections were made using preview data covering only about 7% of the night sky taken prior to the start of the 10-year survey.

The Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile has detected two previously unknown companions orbiting the Milky Way: an ancient ultra-faint dwarf galaxy candidate named Aquarius IV and a dust-obscured globular cluster designated Rubin-GC1. The findings, detailed in research announced by Yale News, stem from preview data collected before the facility began its primary survey operations, providing an early demonstration of the observatory's ability to map sparse stellar structures.

Astronomers currently recognize approximately 60 to 65 satellite galaxies orbiting the Milky Way, but theoretical models suggest hundreds of faint companion systems could remain undiscovered. The new discoveries show that even preliminary testing data from the observatory can isolate faint stellar overdensities that escaped detection in prior sky surveys.

Unearthing Hidden Systems in Early Survey Data

Both systems were detected using the observatory's Early Data Preview 2 (EDP2), a dataset based on observations conducted between April 2025 and January 2026. According to Yale News, the EDP2 release covered roughly 3,000 square degrees, or about 7% of the total night sky. The data was gathered before the facility officially started its 10-year Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) in late June 2026.

Perched atop Cerro Pachón in the Chilean Andes, the Rubin Observatory operates the 8.4-meter Simonyi Survey Telescope equipped with a 3.2-gigapixel camera—the largest digital camera constructed for astronomy, jointly funded by the U.S. National Science Foundation and the U.S. Department of Energy's Office of Science. The camera's wide field of view and high sensitivity allow automated search algorithms to sift through crowded stellar fields and flag diffuse groups of gravitationally bound stars.

Aquarius IV: A Distant Ultra-Faint Dwarf Galaxy Candidate

The first discovery, named Aquarius IV (Rubin J2201−0234), represents the first ultra-faint Milky Way satellite identified directly from Rubin Observatory observations. A research team led by William Cerny, a graduate astronomy researcher at Yale University, detailed the discovery in a study published on arXiv.org and accepted by Research Notes of the AAS.

Astronomers detected the system at 8.0-sigma significance using an implementation of the simple algorithm on the EDP2 stellar catalogs. The researchers subsequently confirmed the presence of the system at approximately 6-sigma significance using archival data from the Dark Energy Camera (DECam) mounted on the Victor M. Blanco 4-meter Telescope at Cerro Tololo, as noted by EarthSky.

Morphological and distance modeling conducted with the ugali toolkit revealed that Aquarius IV is located at a heliocentric distance of approximately 109 kiloparsecs (roughly 355,000 light-years) in the outer Galactic halo. The system has an absolute visual magnitude of -1.9, an azimuthally averaged half-light radius of 19 parsecs (0.60 arcminutes), and an upper limit on ellipticity of 0.61. Fitting its color-magnitude diagram to theoretical models revealed a stellar population consistent with an ancient age of 13 billion years and a very low metallicity (Z = 0.0001). The identification of a likely blue horizontal branch star roughly 0.8 arcminutes from the candidate's center helped refine its distance estimate.

While its physical radius places Aquarius IV in the structural domain occupied by ultra-faint dwarf galaxies, the authors noted that a globular cluster classification cannot yet be completely excluded. Deeper imaging and spectroscopy will be critical to determine its properties and classification at higher confidence.

Rubin-GC1: A Shrouded Star Cluster Tied to Galactic Mergers

The second object, Rubin-GC1 (Rubin J1628-1735), is a compact star cluster situated inside a dust-obscured region of the Milky Way. Led by Aashay Pai of the University of Chicago, the research team reported the detection in a study published on arXiv.org and accepted by The Astrophysical Journal Letters.

Rubin-GC1 was discovered toward the Galactic bulge in the constellation Ophiuchus at Galactic coordinates (l, b) ≈ (359°, 21°). This region presents high stellar background density and substantial interstellar dust extinction, with a foreground reddening value of E(B-V) ≈ 0.7. By simultaneously fitting the system's spatial distribution and stellar isochrones, the researchers determined that Rubin-GC1 has an absolute magnitude of -2.4, an azimuthally averaged half-light radius of approximately 5 parsecs, and a distance of 31 kiloparsecs from the Sun.

The cluster's stellar population has an estimated age of 9.2 billion years and a moderate metallicity of [Fe/H] = -0.73. Analysis of proper motions using data from the European Space Agency's Gaia DR3 catalog revealed a distinct kinematic signature. Comparing its five-dimensional phase-space coordinates to N-body simulations showed that Rubin-GC1 is kinematically associated with the leading tidal arm of the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal galaxy. This indicates that Rubin-GC1 was originally formed outside the Milky Way and later pulled into the halo during the ongoing tidal disruption and accretion of the Sagittarius system.

Why Mapping Faint Milky Way Satellites Matters

Studying ultra-faint stellar systems provides direct empirical tests for theories of dark matter and galactic evolution. As the authors of the Aquarius IV study noted, ultra-faint dwarf galaxies are among the least luminous, oldest, and most dark-matter-dominated structures identified in the cosmos, making them sensitive testing grounds for cosmological models.

Ultra-faint dwarf galaxies are the most dark-matter-dominated galaxies known and serve as probes of galaxy formation and dark matter physics. In addition, finding accreted globular clusters like Rubin-GC1 provides observational records of the hierarchical mergers that built the Milky Way over billions of years.

According to the Aquarius IV research team, search algorithms are projected to identify similar ultra-faint systems with greater than 85% efficiency as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory continues its decade-long survey. With the full LSST mission underway, astronomers expect the observatory to systematically expand the census of companions inhabiting the outer edges of the Galactic halo.

Questions people ask

What did the Vera C. Rubin Observatory discover?

Astronomers analyzing Rubin Observatory Early Data Preview 2 discovered two new Milky Way companions: Aquarius IV, an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy candidate, and Rubin-GC1, a dust-shrouded globular star cluster.

Where are Aquarius IV and Rubin-GC1 located?

Aquarius IV is located in the outer halo of the Milky Way, approximately 355,000 light-years (109 kpc) from Earth. Rubin-GC1 is located about 101,000 light-years (31 kpc) away in the direction of the Galactic bulge in Ophiuchus.

Why is Rubin-GC1 linked to the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy?

Proper motion data from Gaia DR3 and phase-space modeling showed that Rubin-GC1's trajectory matches N-body simulations of the Sagittarius dwarf spheroidal galaxy's leading tidal arm, indicating it was accreted into the Milky Way.

Are Aquarius IV and Rubin-GC1 fully confirmed as galaxies?

Rubin-GC1 is classified as a globular star cluster. Aquarius IV is tentatively classified as an ultra-faint dwarf galaxy based on its size and luminosity, though deeper follow-up spectroscopy is needed to definitively rule out a large star cluster.
