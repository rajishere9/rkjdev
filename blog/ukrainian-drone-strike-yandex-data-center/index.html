Ukrainian Drone Strike Shuts Down Third Major Yandex Data Center

A third precision strike in four days has forced Russian technology giant Yandex to halt operations at its Vladimir campus, disrupting cloud and enterprise systems.

In short

  • A Ukrainian drone strike on October 11, 2026, forced Yandex to completely suspend operations at its 40-to-50-megawatt data center in Vladimir, Russia.
  • The attack is the third hit on Yandex server campuses in four days, following earlier strikes on facilities in Sasovo and Kaluga.
  • Global web monitor NetBlocks reported Yandex Cloud traffic dropped to 30%, while Cloudflare recorded an overnight drop in Yandex bot traffic to 0.4% of peak volume.
  • More than 80 digital platforms experienced disruptions, including artificial intelligence tools, enterprise databases, and commercial retail services.

A Ukrainian drone strike disabled a major Yandex data center in the city of Vladimir on October 11, 2026, forcing a complete operational shutdown of the facility. The strike marks the third attack against the company's core compute infrastructure in four days, following earlier strikes on server hubs in Sasovo and Kaluga. As reported by Kyiv Post, the physical assault triggered cascaded failures across more than 80 consumer applications and cloud infrastructure components.

According to an official service alert cited by The Moscow Times, Yandex confirmed that infrastructure at the Vladimir site east of Moscow was damaged and operations were suspended. While no casualties were reported at the facility, Yandex alerted users on Telegram that the remaining resource configuration was unstable and advised organizations to activate alternate disaster recovery plans while the cloud platform operated in emergency mode.

Three Yandex Data Center Sites Struck in Four Days

The attack in Vladimir represents the latest phase in an expanding interdiction campaign aimed directly at Russian server infrastructure. As documented by Cybernews, the sequence began on October 8, 2026, when Ukrainian strike drones hit Yandex's data center in Sasovo, Ryazan region, roughly 195 miles southeast of Moscow. The Sasovo facility, which caught fire and went offline, housed two of Yandex's three supercomputers used for machine learning and artificial intelligence development.

A day later, on October 9, drones struck a second facility in the regional capital of Kaluga, situated approximately 220 miles west of Sasovo and southwest of Moscow. Yandex confirmed that multiple hardware modules in Kaluga were knocked offline. Following the strikes in Sasovo and Kaluga, Yandex removed the locations of its server facilities across four Russian regions from its online maps platform, according to Kyiv Post.

The Vladimir facility, located in the city's Energetik district, was engineered with a capacity between 40 and 50 megawatts and space for up to 2,880 server racks. Local regional governor Aleksandr Avdeev reported that drone strikes damaged municipal power substations in the area, though utility crews managed to restore 80 percent of electrical service alongside heat and water by Sunday morning. The Russian Defense Ministry claimed its air defenses intercepted 479 fixed-wing drones across 16 regions overnight.

Severe Disruption Across Enterprise and Consumer Systems

The combined damage across the three server campuses has triggered severe operational bottlenecks for Russian digital services. According to global web monitoring organization NetBlocks, cited by Tom's Hardware, network traffic fell to 30% for Yandex Cloud and to 63% for Yandex LLC after the Vladimir attack.

Consumer services that faced disruptions included the Alice voice assistant, Yandex Music, the Telemost videoconferencing platform, and connected smart home appliances, Kyiv Post reported. Hosted business tools on Yandex Cloud suffered extensive outages across essential cloud components, including Compute Cloud, Object Storage, Managed Kubernetes clusters, PostgreSQL, and ClickHouse databases. Developer interfaces and artificial intelligence services such as the YandexGPT API, SpeechKit, and Vision OCR also failed.

The outages quickly extended into ordinary commercial services. Users across Russia encountered failures when attempting to access banking applications, ride-hailing platforms, and major grocery retailers such as Magnit and Fix Price. Earlier in the week, the Sasovo shutdown also disrupted digital infrastructure supporting mobile carrier MegaFon and state rail operator Russian Railways (RZhD).

Global Bot Traffic Collapses Overnight

Beyond domestic commercial disruptions, the physical destruction of Yandex's server architecture produced an immediate contraction in automated internet activity worldwide. Telemetry published by security network provider Cloudflare and detailed by Tom's Hardware showed that bot traffic originating from Yandex networks fell to just 0.4% of its peak two-week volume. The decline began on October 8, when bot volume initially slid to 57% following the Sasovo strike, before dropping further once Kaluga and Vladimir were knocked offline.

Alternative backup options remain limited for Russian organizations. Commercial satellite internet options are largely unavailable inside the country: Starlink does not operate in Russia, and the domestic satellite constellation, Rassvet, experienced major setbacks after a significant portion of its satellites de-orbited post-launch, according to Tom's Hardware.

Financially, Yandex, which commands roughly 70% of the domestic search market and held an estimated market capitalization of $16 billion as of October 2026, saw its stock price fall approximately 4% on the Moscow Exchange earlier in the week, according to Cybernews and Kyiv Post.

Retaliatory Dynamic and Physical Infrastructure Threats

Ukrainian officials framed the strikes as direct retaliation for repeated Russian attacks against Ukrainian internet infrastructure. Following the Sasovo incident, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy stated that Kyiv is responding in mirror-like fashion to ongoing Russian strikes on Ukrainian digital hubs, as reported by Al Jazeera. Over the preceding month, Russian strikes targeted Ukrainian telecommunications installations in central Kyiv, causing outages for several websites and prompting local firms to transfer servers to overseas facilities.

As reported by BBC News, the strikes on Yandex occurred amid broader geopolitical friction, including pressure from international leaders over attacks on Russian oil refineries. Ukrainian defense analysts maintain that targeting digital centers disrupts the logistics sustaining the war effort, while forcing Moscow to divert resources toward repairing and defending domestic enterprise assets.

The vulnerability of enterprise computing centers to aerial weapons highlights an expanding operational pattern. As noted by Tom's Hardware, custom-engineered drones exceeding ranges of 1,500 miles are capable of destroying Russian Tu-95 strategic bombers parked deep behind front lines, while strikes specifically targeting data centers represent a newer development. With Yandex acknowledging that it remains very difficult to predict when full compute operations can resume, Russian businesses face an extended period of degraded cloud capacity and infrastructure instability.

Questions people ask

Which Yandex data center facilities have been struck by drones?

Three data centers were struck between October 8 and October 11, 2026: an AI supercomputer facility in Sasovo (Ryazan region), a server hub in Kaluga, and a 40-to-50-megawatt data center in Vladimir.

What services went offline following the Vladimir data center strike?

More than 80 services experienced disruptions, including Yandex Cloud compute infrastructure, managed database services, artificial intelligence APIs like YandexGPT, the Alice voice assistant, and third-party consumer systems such as banking and retail applications.

How did the attacks affect international web traffic?

Telemetry reported by Cloudflare showed that automated bot traffic originating from Yandex networks plummeted to 0.4% of its peak two-week volume following the series of strikes.
