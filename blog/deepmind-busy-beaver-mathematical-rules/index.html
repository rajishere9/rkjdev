DeepMind AI Finds Mathematical Rules in Busy Beaver Problem

A research team used Gemini 3.1 Pro and the Lean 4 proof assistant to extract and verify exact dynamical rules for 11 previously unanalyzed six-state Turing machines.

In short

  • Google DeepMind researchers developed an agent pipeline using Gemini 3.1 Pro to analyze holdout Turing machines in the Busy Beaver problem.
  • The system discovered and verified compact mathematical acceleration rules in Lean 4 for 11 previously unanalyzed six-state machines.
  • The 11 machines are provisionally classified as 'Cryptids' because their executions reduce to unresolved Collatz-like problems.
  • The pipeline does not resolve whether the machines halt, and the authors note that discovering brand-new mathematical structures beyond Collatz-style patterns remains an open goal.

In a research paper submitted to arXiv.org on October 15, 2026, researchers at Google DeepMind reported that an artificial intelligence system has discovered and formally verified new mathematical structures within the Busy Beaver problem. According to the researchers, the work demonstrates how language model agents can move beyond answering static benchmark questions to extract compact algebraic rules directly from uncomputable computational systems.

Authored by Marco Dos Santos, Vivek Veeriah, Alex Havrilla, Andrew Bolt, Goran Zuzic, Satinder Singh, and Tom Zahavy, the paper introduces a pipeline that analyzes the execution traces of six-state Turing machines. Rather than simply evaluating candidate solutions to known problems, the Gemini-based agent inferred higher-level dynamics from raw tape executions and wrote machine-checked proofs to verify them.

How Gemini Analyzed the Busy Beaver Problem

The Busy Beaver problem focuses on finding the maximum number of steps an n-state Turing machine can execute before halting on an initially blank tape. The value, denoted as BB(n), is noncomputable in general. As detailed by OddBrief, the community settled the fifth Busy Beaver number, BB(5), through a collaborative mathematical effort that did not rely on AI, shifting analytical attention to the remaining holdouts in the six-state domain, BB(6).

To tackle these holdout machines, the DeepMind team developed an agentic workflow powered by Gemini 3.1 Pro. The core technique treats the read-write history of a Turing machine tape as a dynamical system, as reported by sina.cn. The pipeline first simulates the target machine to look for repeating patterns on its tape. Gemini 3.1 Pro is then prompted to conjecture an "acceleration"—a concise mathematical rule that computes the result of many operational steps at once.

Candidate rules must predict the recorded simulation steps with 100 percent accuracy before advancing. Once an acceleration matches the simulation data, Gemini generates an informal mathematical proof. A subsequent agent then translates that argument into the Lean 4 interactive theorem prover. According to the preprint, the research team manually verified the Lean formal statements, and the resulting proofs have been made public.

Classifying 11 New Cryptid Machines in BB(6)

The team evaluated their pipeline against candidate machines compiled by the theoretical computer science community. The broad search analyzed 1,094 BB(6) holdouts assembled by community member mxdys, alongside 65 holdouts from a related set and four from another source, according to OddBrief.

The search filtered the initial pool down to 52 candidate machines, 50 of which were six-state machines. From that group, the automated pipeline generated novel, formally verified accelerations for 11 machines that lacked any prior documented analysis. The authors also produced formal proofs or verified higher-level rules for 15 machines whose behaviors had previously been discussed in informal community forums, such as Discord, but had never been verified.

The authors have provisionally classified these 11 machines as "Cryptids," pending formal review by the Busy Beaver research community. In the terminology of the paper, a Cryptid is a Turing machine whose blank-tape trajectory admits an exact, compact mathematical formulation, but whose ultimate halting state remains undetermined.

Open Limits and the Halting Problem

While the system successfully summarized long sequences of machine transitions into mathematical statements, it did not resolve whether the 11 machines actually halt. As noted by Welcome.AI, the mathematical rules discovered by the agent reduce the machines' behavior into Collatz-like problems. These branching arithmetic sequences mirror the long-standing Collatz conjecture in number theory, which remains unsolved.

The researchers highlighted explicit boundaries in their current setup. Because the approach only looks for Collatz-like structure that mathematicians already study, the exploration is not entirely open-ended. The authors noted that any potential insight into the broader Collatz conjecture itself remains speculative. The next objective outlined by the team is developing autonomous architectures capable of recognizing novel categories of open mathematical questions concealed in computational traces, rather than searching exclusively for familiar structures.

Questions people ask

Did Google DeepMind solve the Busy Beaver BB(6) value?

No. The system did not decide whether the six-state machines halt. It derived and proved mathematical rules that simplify their behavior into Collatz-like sequences, but the halting status of those sequences remains unsolved.

What is a 'Cryptid' Turing machine?

In the authors' terminology, a Cryptid is a Turing machine whose execution on a blank tape can be expressed through an exact, compact mathematical rule, yet its ultimate halting behavior remains unknown.

Which AI models and tools were used in the study?

The research team utilized Gemini 3.1 Pro to propose conjectures and write informal arguments, paired with an agent that constructed formal, verified proofs inside the Lean 4 theorem prover.
