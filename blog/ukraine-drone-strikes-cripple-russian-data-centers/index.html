Ukrainian Drone Strikes Damage Russian Data Centers and Slow AI Work

A coordinated aerial campaign damaged server hubs run by Yandex, Sberbank, and VKontakte, triggering cross-border service outages and hobbling domestic AI development.

In short

  • Long-range Ukrainian drone strikes in October 2026 hit five major computing hubs, damaging three Yandex facilities, a Sberbank AI center, and a VKontakte site.
  • The strikes knocked out two of Yandex's three supercomputers in Sasovo, Ryazan Oblast, damaging hardware powered by Nvidia A100 processors.
  • Disruptions rippled through Russian ride-hailing, payments, arbitration courts, and automated bot networks, extending to services in at least eight foreign countries.
  • Industry analysts report that sanctions and global chip shortages could make physical server repairs take months or years.

A series of Ukrainian drone strikes on critical Russian computing facilities throughout October 2026 has caused widespread digital outages and dealt a direct blow to the country's artificial intelligence development. The aerial campaign hit infrastructure belonging to search and cloud provider Yandex, state lender Sberbank, and social network VKontakte, knocking out advanced supercomputers and exposing the vulnerability of Russia's centralized server network.

According to reporting by Fortune, Ukrainian drones damaged three key data centers operated by Yandex, one Sberbank facility, and one VKontakte location over an eleven-day span. While Russian corporate operators have remained guarded regarding the total structural destruction, commercial users, transit riders, and automated platforms across the region reported widespread system failures.

Wave of Drone Attacks Knocks Core Sites Offline

The targeted campaign began on the night of October 7 to October 8, 2026, when drones struck Yandex's primary server campus in Sasovo, located in Russia's Ryazan region. In an official statement released on October 9, Yandex confirmed that the Sasovo site was seriously damaged in the attack and forced into a complete shutdown, adding that no personnel were injured. Satellite imagery captured on October 20 by Planet Labs PBC and reviewed by The Washington Post confirmed heavy damage across the Sasovo facility.

Subsequent strikes followed rapidly. Yandex's hub in Kaluga was hit on the morning of October 9, disabling part of its operating infrastructure, while a third facility in Vladimir suffered an attack on the night of October 11 that halted all local operations. Reporting from The Kyiv Independent noted that local monitoring groups recorded further strikes targeting the Vladimir site on October 16 amid defensive fire and airport closures around Moscow.

The strikes expanded beyond Yandex in mid-October. On October 14, Ukrainian drones hit Sberbank's data center and artificial intelligence hub in Balakovo, situated in the Saratov region. Four days later, on October 18, another strike hit a VKontakte computing hub in St. Petersburg, as reported by Fortune. Russian regional air defenses struggled to shield the sprawling facilities, with Eurasia Group analyst Nick Reiners telling Fortune that Russia faces a basic resource dilemma in attempting to protect massive geographic territory with limited interceptors.

Impact on Consumer Services and Disinformation Operations

Severe damage to data center equipment and computing infrastructure prompted acute disruptions across Russian municipal and consumer services. Yandex operates pervasive applications covering web search, payments, e-commerce, cloud storage, and transit. Following the strikes, Russian residents reported failures in Yandex Market orders and taxi dispatch connections, while Yandex online banking services and Yandex's voice assistant Alisa experienced major outages. The disruptions extended into the judicial system, where regional arbitration courts were forced to postpone hearings and lost access to legal databases.

The impact also crossed international boundaries. An analysis by the Atlantic Council documented outages of Yandex services in at least eight nations across Central Asia and Eastern Europe, prompting emergency intervention from Kazakhstan's Ministry of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Development to assist in local service restorations.

Digital disinformation networks also showed quantifiable drops in output. According to an analytical report by Ukraine's Center for Countering Disinformation, tracking of 5,201 accounts displaying inauthentic behavior on Facebook and Telegram showed an 83.6 percent drop in message volume on October 8 following the Sasovo strike, falling from 4,032 messages to 660. While traffic rebounded over subsequent days, message volume plunged by another 42.6 percent on October 12 following the shutdown of the Vladimir facility.

Severe Setback for Russian Artificial Intelligence

While consumer software and web searches were gradually rerouted to surviving backup sites, hardware damage to specialized research clusters presents a much steeper obstacle for domestic industry. The Sasovo facility housed two of Yandex's three deep-learning supercomputers, which are ranked among the top 500 globally, including a flagship cluster ranked 19th in the world.

Industry specialists caution that recovering this computing capacity will prove difficult under international trade restrictions. Connor Martin, a researcher with the Council on Foreign Relations, stated in a commentary cited by Fortune that Russia faces steep hurdles in replacing the U.S.-designed Nvidia A100 graphics processors that run Yandex's premier machine-learning clusters. Some observers noted that fires triggered by the attacks probably seriously damaged high-end chips, and Klimarev stated that "soot from the fire is bad for electronics."

"This effectively sets Russia far behind in the AI race," Mikhail Klimarev, director of the Internet Protection Society, told Fortune. Klimarev pointed out that persistent global shortages of advanced accelerator hardware and stringent export controls mean repairs could take months, years, or remain unachievable under current market constraints.

Data Sovereignty Laws Amplify Physical Vulnerabilities

The strikes have laid bare the structural risks of Russia's digital sovereignty mandates. In 2015, the Russian government enacted strict laws requiring that all personal data belonging to Russian citizens be physically hosted on servers located within national borders. While Ukraine distributed its state records and critical data across cloud servers located in allied European countries to prevent physical annihilation, Moscow's policy forced domestic tech companies to concentrate enormous volumes of compute and storage inside a handful of domestic mega facilities.

Because Yandex concentrated the bulk of its operational workloads in just six major facilities, knocking out three campuses within four days severely stretched the company's redundancy plans. Atlantic Council analysts noted that digital infrastructure must now be defended like electric generation facilities, as centralized server farms represent highly concentrated points of physical vulnerability during modern industrial conflicts.

Questions people ask

Which data centers were attacked in the October 2026 strikes?

Ukrainian drones targeted three facilities operated by Yandex located in Sasovo (Ryazan region), Kaluga, and Vladimir, as well as a Sberbank AI data hub in Balakovo and a VKontakte server center in St. Petersburg.

How did the strikes affect Russia's artificial intelligence research?

The attack on Yandex's Sasovo center knocked out two of its three primary supercomputers, which used Nvidia A100 processors. Western sanctions and global component shortages make replacing these high-end chips extremely difficult, significantly setting back domestic model development.

Why were Russian data centers particularly vulnerable to centralized attacks?

Federal digital sovereignty regulations enacted since 2015 require all Russian personal data to remain inside the country, causing companies like Yandex to centralize vast operations into a small number of mega facilities within range of long-distance drones.
