Anthropic Restricts Claude From Reverse-Engineering Video Games

Prompt guardrails added to Claude Code and Opus 5.5 aim to stop software cloning, but legitimate game developers report collateral refusals.

In short

  • Anthropic added a strict directive to Claude Code on Opus 5.5 instructing the model to reject recreating specific existing games.
  • The restriction followed rampant AI-assisted game cloning, demo scraping fears, and reverse-engineering projects across the gaming sector.
  • Legitimate developers report that the guardrail is misfiring on routine troubleshooting, game mechanics, and tabletop game patches.

Anthropic has implemented system-level prompt restrictions to stop its Claude AI models from being used to decompile, mod, and clone video games. The change, pushed to Claude Code running on the Opus 5.5 model in late October 2026, aims to curb an influx of unauthorized software clones and protect commercial intellectual property.

According to reporting from Game Developer, users first noticed the modification on October 27, filed under an internal rule identifier labeled "tengu_heron_brook." The directive instructs the assistant: "Do build original games, games in a genre, and games from the user’s own designs and assets. Do not build a recreation of a specific existing game, even if it is renamed or called original."

Addy Osmani, a member of Anthropic's technical staff, confirmed the policy update on social platform X, noting that the team inserted two sentences into Claude Code's prompt on Opus 5.5. Osmani explained that using the model to infringe on intellectual property violates Anthropic's usage guidelines, while clarifying that original software designs and genre concepts remain permitted.

The Catalysts Behind the Crackdown

The move arrives amid rising anxiety across the video game industry over automated code scraping and fast-follow cloning. As reported by PC Gamer, independent game developers have grown alarmed by low-effort, AI-assisted imitations flooding storefronts like Valve's Steam. During the most recent Steam Next Fest, demo submissions doubled compared to the previous edition, with AI-generated titles driving much of the surge.

The rapid capabilities of Opus 5.5 also exacerbated reverse-engineering activities. Mashable reported that community hobbyists and bad actors used AI to accelerate game decompilation. Users ported titles such as World of Warcraft, Call of Duty, and Grand Theft Auto: Vice City directly into web browsers without developer authorization. In other instances, modders merged mechanics from disparate titles, placing Nintendo's Mario inside Elden Ring or inserting Spider-Man into Batman: Arkham City.

More pressing for platform holders, reverse-engineering workflows assisted a jailbreak of the PlayStation 5 console. That exploit allowed cross-platform play and unauthorized execution of console exclusives, including running Halo 2 and The Legend of Zelda: Breath of the Wild on Sony hardware. Coupled with legal fallout surrounding viral leaks of Grand Theft Auto VI, which led Take-Two Interactive to serve subpoenas to Discord and Microsoft, frontier AI developers faced mounting pressure to curtail infringement vectors.

Independent studios have altered their public roadmaps in response to AI-assisted asset theft. Studio Digital Sun Games recently canceled an upcoming public playtest of its game ReVamp, fearing unauthorized parties would feed the demo code into an automated model to generate a copycat release before the studio could ship the final product. Tom's Hardware also documented instances of extortion scams targeting developers with threats of duplicating unfinished game demos.

False Positives Disrupt Legitimate Programmers

While the updated policy targets bad actors, legitimate software engineers are facing unexpected disruptions. As Tom's Hardware reported, Claude Code's GitHub repository has recorded an influx of issue tickets detailing refusals on ordinary development tasks.

Developers report that innocuous workflows—including debugging code midway through an original game's production, requesting help to implement common mechanics, patching tabletop-style games, and utilizing legitimate decompiling utilities like the Reverse Engineer Anything (REA) tool—are being blocked by Claude's new prompt filters. The blanket nature of the rule makes it difficult for the model to differentiate between genuine intellectual property theft and standard engineering practices.

Addressing the complaints, Osmani acknowledged the friction on X, stating that Anthropic intends to iterate to balance safety and utility. "In the meantime, if Claude turns down something that's yours, send it my way," Osmani wrote.

Platform Responses and the Open-Weight Question

Platform distributors have struggled to craft concrete policies around AI-generated software clones. At Steam's Indie Connect event, Valve's Tom Giardino acknowledged that Steam faces an issue with low-effort AI titles. However, Giardino stated that Valve will not ban AI coding assistants outright, explaining that the company does not want to act as a gatekeeper dictating developer toolsets. Instead, Giardino advised creators to protect their work by emphasizing personal perspective, distinct creativity, and ongoing feature updates.

Observers have also pointed out industry ironies and enforcement loopholes. As Tom's Hardware contributor Bruno Ferreira highlighted, frontier AI developers that scraped public internet data to train foundational models are now tasked with preventing copyright violations from their own users.

Furthermore, prompt-level restrictions on closed models may do little to deter determined bad actors. Generative AI filters can frequently be bypassed using rhetorical prompting techniques, and programmers seeking to reverse-engineer software can easily pivot away from Claude toward uncensored, open-weight models that lack centralized usage controls.
