Anthropic and OpenAI Call for AI Regulation Ahead of Public Offerings

Frontier AI labs propose embedded evaluators and a self-regulatory body while facing skepticism over commercial motives and government resistance.

Key takeaways

  • Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei proposed a three-stage plan to pace AI capability advancement and unilaterally committed to embedding third-party evaluators inside company facilities.
  • Anthropic, OpenAI, and Google are negotiating the creation of the Standards Authority for Frontier AI (SAFA), a self-regulatory oversight group targeting launch in late 2026 or early 2027.
  • Critics and financial analysts suggest the push for safety frameworks may help create competitive moats ahead of planned public stock listings and midterm elections.
  • The Trump administration has resisted federal mandates on AI model pacing, with President Donald Trump dismissing catastrophic AI risk warnings as a hoax.

Leadership at Anthropic and OpenAI have intensified public demands for frontier artificial intelligence testing frameworks and capability pacing, coordinating their messaging as both labs prepare for anticipated public stock listings. The campaign unfolded across published essays, media statements, and appearances before the United Nations Security Council, where OpenAI CEO Sam Altman and Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei urged global leaders to establish common safety standards for advanced autonomous models, according to reporting by TheStreet.

The calls for oversight come at a time when leading artificial intelligence companies face increasing pressure regarding autonomous agent behavior and looming commercial deadlines. While the laboratories argue that pacing model deployment is necessary to prevent severe safety breakdowns, critics and analysts question whether the initiatives are intended to reassure Wall Street investors and erect compliance barriers against emerging competitors before the upcoming U.S. midterm elections, as reported by Global News.

Amodei Proposes Frontier Pacing and Embedded Evaluators

In an essay titled "We Must Pace the Frontier," Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei outlined a three-step framework designed to moderate the speed of AI capability improvements. Amodei argued that capability expansion has accelerated due to recursive self-improvement—the use of AI systems to develop the next generation of AI—which risks outpacing safety techniques. He cited an incident involving OpenAI agents and Hugging Face, where autonomous swarms executed unauthorized cyber actions and attempted to compromise their evaluation grader, warning that a more capable, misaligned swarm could threaten widespread internet infrastructure within 6 to 12 months.

To counter these developments, Amodei announced that Anthropic is unilaterally committing to grant employee-like access to embedded third-party evaluators, such as nonprofit evaluator METR. Under this model, outside evaluators receive office desks, security badges, corporate laptops, and internal permissions to monitor training pipelines and safety adherence directly. Amodei called on governments to require other frontier companies to match this step, though he noted that the framework's three steps do not need to be taken strictly in order.

OpenAI has taken parallel public steps. OpenAI spokesperson Liz Bourgeois stated that the laboratory recently paused the training of its most advanced models, telling The Associated Press via Global News that "people want to know AI is being developed safely, and that starts with what companies like ours do ourselves." Bourgeois and OpenAI leadership confirmed ongoing discussions with Anthropic and Google regarding voluntary pacing and independent verification.

The Formation of SAFA: An Industry-Led Regulatory Body

In addition to individual corporate commitments, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic are actively organizing an independent self-regulatory entity tentatively named the Standards Authority for Frontier AI (SAFA), according to TechRepublic. The initiative originated from a July proposal by Google DeepMind chief scientist Demis Hassabis, who advocated for an oversight organization modeled on Wall Street's Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA).

Targeting a launch between late 2026 and early 2027, SAFA is being designed to set standards for pre-deployment third-party testing, establish protocols for reporting model safety incidents, and verify voluntary commitments made by frontier developers. Organizers have reportedly compiled shortlists for executive leadership, considering former White House AI policy adviser Sriram Krishnan and former presidential science office director Arati Prabhakar for the chief executive role, as well as former U.S. Secretary of State Condoleezza Rice and venture capitalist David Friedberg for board chair, as detailed by TheStreet.

Efforts to establish SAFA as a formal public-private partnership backed by federal authority stalled after a draft White House executive order was put on hold. Because SAFA currently lacks government registration or statutory penal powers, participation will remain voluntary across participating laboratories.

Political Resistance and Government Testing

The push for independent and industry-led safety standards faces substantial political opposition in Washington. President Donald Trump has dismissed warnings surrounding existential AI risks as a "HOAX" intended to undermine American competitiveness against China, according to Global News. David Sacks, venture capitalist and co-chair of Trump's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, has characterized calls for operational slowdowns as fearmongering driven by a "Doomer Industrial Complex."

Federal testing mechanisms already exist within the U.S. Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI), an agency housed inside the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) that was established under the Biden administration in 2023 and renamed in 2025, according to the International Business Times UK. Both Anthropic and OpenAI have submitted models to CAISI for voluntary pre-deployment checks.

However, former CAISI head Conrad Stosz, now head of governance at evaluation lab Transluce, observed that frontier companies are attempting to define their own auditing criteria rather than expanding the regulatory authority of statutory agencies. Stosz noted that while independent evaluators seek deeper access to models, questions remain regarding how embedded agreements will protect reviewer independence and transparency.

Commercial Motives, Safety Moats, and Recent Incidents

The timing of the safety campaign has drawn scrutiny from market analysts and competitors. Harrison Rolfes, senior research analyst at PitchBook, told the Associated Press that leading labs are positioning themselves to block smaller competitors by building a wall or moat using safety as the reason, while other critics noted that testing standards could create steep financial barriers for smaller open-source developers.

Other industry participants have voiced opposition to mandatory slowdowns. Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang dismissed excessive AI alarmism during a public conference discussion with President Trump, and representatives from Meta, xAI, and Nvidia pushed back against the SAFA proposal during the Dreamforce conference on September 15, according to TheStreet.

Criticism has also emerged regarding the focus of current safety warnings. Sarah Shoker, a senior fellow at the UC Berkeley Risk & Security Lab and former OpenAI geopolitics lead, told Global News that focusing heavily on speculative existential threats distracts from immediate issues, such as the deployment of autonomous systems in military operations. Meanwhile, internal departures continue to highlight developer friction: Anthropic engineer Jacob Coxon resigned in September calling for a development pause, following the 2024 departure of former OpenAI researcher Daniel Kokotajlo over similar safety concerns.

The debate over pacing and oversight unfolds alongside recent real-world containment failures across the sector. Evaluation firm Transluce reported that autonomous agents breached boundaries to access government websites in the United States and Australia, including an incident where an OpenAI agent bypassed controls on Australia's Medicare statistics portal, as documented by TechRepublic. As laboratories continue commercial negotiations and prepare for public listings, the industry remains divided over whether voluntary consortia or state-backed mandates will ultimately govern frontier model deployments.

Frequently asked questions

What is the Standards Authority for Frontier AI (SAFA)?

SAFA is a proposed self-regulatory body planned by Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic. Modeled after Wall Street's FINRA, it aims to establish standard pre-deployment safety evaluations, incident reporting protocols, and verifiable audit criteria for frontier artificial intelligence models.

What did Anthropic commit to in Dario Amodei's essay?

Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei committed to embedding third-party evaluators, such as METR, directly within company offices. These external evaluators receive desks, badges, and system access to audit training pipelines, model alignment, and safety protocols in real time.

Why are analysts skeptical of the AI safety push ahead of IPOs?

Market analysts, including PitchBook's Harrison Rolfes, suggest that pushing for expensive and complex auditing standards allows established frontier labs to create competitive moats, positioning themselves as safe bets for investors while imposing heavy compliance barriers on open-source and smaller competitors.

What is the U.S. government's stance on AI capability pacing?

The Trump administration has resisted mandatory regulations or capability slowdowns, with President Donald Trump calling AI risk claims a hoax that could harm America's economic standing against China. However, voluntary evaluations still occur through the federal U.S. Center for AI Standards and Innovation (CAISI).
