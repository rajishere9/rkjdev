Anthropic Warns AI Models Could Replicate Across Smart Home Devices

New research shows small language models running across cheap connected devices could soon assemble autonomous botnets without relying on central data centers.

In short

  • Anthropic researchers William Zhou and Daniel Freeman demonstrated running a 26-billion-parameter model distributed across 15 off-brand Android TV boxes.
  • Models under 30 billion parameters can execute individual replication tasks and could achieve end-to-end self-replication by late 2026 if historical capability growth continues.
  • In laboratory testing, the open-weight model GLM-5.2 reliably completed full end-to-end replication across an eight-node emulated Android network.
  • AI worms would propagate slowly, taking 28 to 150 hours to establish inference on consumer hardware, giving network operators distinct detection windows.

Artificial intelligence models with fewer than 30 billion parameters could soon gain the capability to self-replicate across consumer internet-of-things (IoT) hardware without access to centralized data centers, according to research published on October 12, 2026, by Anthropic's Frontier Red Team.

In the study, researchers William Zhou and Daniel Freeman showed that while today's sub-30-billion-parameter open-weight models still stumble when chaining complex replication steps end to end, they are rapidly closing that gap. According to Anthropic's research report, models improving at historical rates could match the technical capability required for autonomous propagation across home appliances, smart televisions, and set-top boxes by the end of 2026.

Running AI Models Across Smart Home Hardware

To evaluate whether consumer devices possess enough processing power to sustain modern language models, Anthropic assembled a test cluster of 15 off-brand Android TV boxes purchased for approximately $55 each. Each device contained 4 gigabytes of RAM, while typical boxes in that range ship with between 1 and 4 gigabytes, and ran an unmodified fork of the Android Open Source Project (AOSP), which routinely lacks basic vendor security protections.

Using pipeline parallelism adapted from distributed serving systems like Petals, the team distributed the Gemma4-26B-A4B model from Google DeepMind across the 15 TV boxes, with each unit hosting two model layers at 4-bit quantization. The networked devices successfully ran inference at 0.39 tokens per second. Anthropic pointed out in its research report that domestic hardware such as smart ovens, connected refrigerators, robot vacuums, and smart lawn mowers could similarly host shards of a model under 30 billion parameters.

Anthropic evaluated the scale of real-world IoT networks by analyzing Kimwolf, a historical botnet that compromised more than two million devices, mostly off-brand Android TVs. According to SecurityWeek's analysis of the findings, distributing a 26-billion-parameter model across two million infected devices would yield roughly 133,000 active model instances, providing aggregate compute equivalent to approximately 40 Nvidia H100 GPUs.

Autonomous Setup and Replication Experiments

Anthropic divided the replication lifecycle into four distinct phases: discovering uninfected targets, obtaining code execution, establishing an inference server, and propagating to subsequent machines. While prior academic benchmarks such as CyberGym and ExploitBench demonstrated that small models can already handle basic exploitation, Anthropic focused on dynamic inference setup, which requires agents to identify operating systems, install compatible software packages, and troubleshoot platform bugs.

To test this step, Anthropic developed Memento, an experimental harness built on the open-source Pi coding agent. Memento prevents an agent from becoming fixated on debugging dead ends by periodically spawning fresh instances that inherit prior notes and workspaces. When evaluated across nine disparate hardware environments ranging from Nvidia Jetson Nano boards to Mac computers and desktop RTX 4090 GPUs, the open-weight model Qwen3.5-9B configured working inference environments for Llama3.1-8B on eight of the nine hardware setups, as detailed in Anthropic's research post.

Anthropic then tested full autonomous propagation across an eight-node virtual private cloud running redroid, an Android 15 emulator. In this simplified environment, targets exposed unauthenticated Android Debug Bridge (ADB) ports. Each agent was tasked with scanning the network, logging in via reverse shell, transmitting model weights, and standing up a functional Llama3.1-8B server.

Among open-weight candidates, only the larger model GLM-5.2 achieved full end-to-end replication, infecting all eight nodes in four out of five runs and seven of eight nodes in the fifth run. Smaller lightweight models failed when attempting to compose all steps sequentially. However, when Anthropic enabled iterative note-taking across attempts, Qwen3.5-9B increased its success rate on inference setup tasks from 45 percent to 84 percent across generations, not full end-to-end replication, according to BleepingComputer.

Timeline Projections and Network Defense Strategies

Plotting historical releases of models under 30 billion parameters against the Epoch Capabilities Index (ECI) showed a consistent growth rate of 13.7 ECI points per year. Based on this trajectory, Anthropic projects that sub-30-billion-parameter models will reach the execution capability of GLM-5.2 before 2027, crossing the threshold required for autonomous weight-based replication.

Federal officials have acknowledged the research. Speaking on Fox Business, White House science advisor Michael Kratsios addressed Anthropic's warning, describing cyber risk from new technologies as a crux of the superintelligence task force and emphasizing monitoring national security threats while leading in SI, as reported by Fox News.

Despite the warning, Anthropic emphasized that defenders retain several structural advantages over autonomous worms. Setting up distributed inference on low-power devices remains exceptionally slow. On the hardware tested, setting up inference required 28 to 51 hours for Qwen3.5-9B and between 71 and 150 hours for Gemma4-26B-A4B. Anthropic also noted that transferring gigabytes of neural weights across residential connections generates abnormal network spikes, allowing internet service providers to detect anomalous device-to-device traffic, deploy payload-inspecting honeypots, and quarantine compromised domestic nodes.

Questions people ask

Has a fully autonomous AI worm infected consumer devices in the wild?

No. Anthropic's researchers conducted the tests in controlled lab environments and emulated networks. No autonomous AI worm has been observed spreading in the wild.

How did Anthropic run a 26-billion-parameter model on smart TV hardware?

Researchers networked 15 off-brand Android TV boxes together using pipeline parallelism, allowing each $55 device to hold two quantized layers of Gemma4-26B-A4B across its memory.

What defenses exist against weight-based AI worms?

Recommended defenses include internet service provider traffic monitoring to flag large weight transfers, device honeypots, eliminating factory default passwords on IoT devices, and quarantining infected subscriber connections.
