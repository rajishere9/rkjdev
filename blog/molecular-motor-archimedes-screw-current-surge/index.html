Molecular Motor Inspired by Archimedes' Screw Boosts Current 100-Fold

An international research team has developed an electrically driven helical molecule that spins in both directions and multiplies electrical conductivity during continuous rotation.

In short

  • An international team built a molecular motor by suspending a helical molecule between two microscopic gold electrodes.
  • Applying an electrical current causes electrons to transfer angular momentum to the molecule, driving reversible rotation.
  • Continuous rotation increases the molecule's electrical conductivity by up to two orders of magnitude (100-fold).
  • Supercomputer simulations revealed that rotation forces the molecule into conductive geometric positions it avoids when stationary.
  • The device is a laboratory prototype that demonstrates a new mechanism for molecular electronics, spintronics, and microfluidics.

In a study published in Nature in October 2026, an international research team demonstrated a molecular motor that uses an electric current to spin a single helical molecule suspended between two metal electrodes. The tiny mechanism, inspired by the ancient Archimedes screw, spins in a direction determined by the polarity of the applied voltage. When the rotation reaches a continuous threshold, the molecule's electrical conductivity spikes by up to 100 times, revealing an unexpected physical link between mechanical movement and electrical transport at the atomic scale.

The experimental research was led by Dr. Anil Kumar Singh during his postdoctoral work under Prof. Oren Tal in the Department of Chemical and Biological Physics at the Weizmann Institute of Science. Singh has since joined the Indian Institute of Technology Madras as a faculty member. Collaborating institutions included the University of Copenhagen, the University of Augsburg, the Niels Bohr Institute, and Huazhong University of Science and Technology.

Adapting an Ancient Machine to the Nanoscale

The Archimedes screw was conceived more than 2,000 years ago as an inclined mechanical cylinder encasing a spiral blade to lift water uphill. In recent decades, engineers have also adapted the mechanism in reverse to generate power as hydroelectric turbines. The new research adapts this geometry to the single-molecule regime, as reported by uni-augsburg.de. Instead of water moving through a mechanical auger, a stream of electrons passes through a helical molecule, driving rotational movement.

Electrically driven molecular motors were first demonstrated in 2011, but earlier designs were anchored to a single surface and required the tip of a scanning tunneling microscope to induce motion. The newly documented architecture represents the first molecular motor firmly bound across two metal contacts that can rotate in a controllable direction under an applied current, according to ScienceDaily.

Building and Testing the Molecular Motor

The physical machine centers on a synthesized helical organic molecule known as helicene, created by Prof. Michael Pittelkow's research group at the University of Copenhagen. Measuring a few millionths of a millimeter across, the spiral molecule terminates with sulfur atoms at each end. These sulfur atoms anchor the molecule securely to gold surfaces, as described by ScienceDaily.

At the Weizmann Institute, Tal and Singh mounted single molecules between two sharpened gold electrodes tapered to atomic dimensions at temperatures near absolute zero. By adjusting the distance between these two tips, the experimenters could physically stretch or compress the helical structure. According to the team, varying the mechanical spacing functions similarly to adjusting the pitch or blade angles of a turbine.

When the researchers applied an electrical voltage across the junction, electrons moved through the spiral molecule. Because of the molecule's chiral geometry, the electrons transfer angular momentum to the atomic framework as they pass through, driving it into rotation. Measurements showed that switching the polarity of the voltage reversed the direction of the rotation.

The Mechanism Behind the 100-Fold Current Surge

During the initial electrical tests, the researchers observed cyclical current jumps indicating sequential rotational steps. When the voltage crossed a specific threshold, however, the current surged abruptly by up to 100 times—a two-orders-of-magnitude increase documented by Hayadan. Furthermore, the voltage threshold at which this current surge occurred shifted whenever researchers compressed or stretched the molecule between the gold tips.

Because laboratory instruments can only record current across the nanocontact rather than visually recording atomic positions, theoretical physicists were enlisted to model the interaction. Prof. Fabian Pauly, along with doctoral researchers Léon Martin and Yuta Ito at the Chair of Theoretical Physics I at the University of Augsburg, carried out quantum mechanical simulations using Augsburg's LiCCA supercomputer.

The simulations revealed that the electrical conductivity of the helical molecule depends directly on its rotational angle. When stationary at rest, the molecule naturally settles into positions with low conductance. When the electrical driving force exceeds the threshold and forces continuous rotation, the molecule enters transient geometric orientations that it otherwise avoids.

"The surprising thing is that it is precisely the positions that the molecule avoids when at rest that conduct electrons best. It is only the rotation that makes these positions accessible," Martin explained in the institutional report from uni-augsburg.de.

Working with theoretical physicists Per Hedegård from the Niels Bohr Institute and Jing-Tao Lü from Huazhong University of Science and Technology, the Augsburg team developed a simplified theoretical model that reproduced the transition from stepped rotation to continuous rotation and the accompanying 100-fold conductivity surge.

What the Discovery Means for Molecular Electronics

Nature regularly employs molecular motors within biological cells to convert energy and generate movement, an insight highlighted during the awarding of the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Synthetically replicating that utility has remained an active target of research, as noted by analytica-world.com.

"We have shown that an electric current can be used to generate a directed rotation and that this rotation, in turn, controls the current. This is a new operating principle for machines at the very limits of miniaturisation," Pauly said in the study's release.

The authors emphasize that the current device remains a laboratory prototype evaluated under controlled experimental conditions, including near-absolute-zero temperatures. Practical commercial implementations have not yet been demonstrated. Nonetheless, according to reporting from english.news.cn, researchers anticipate the mechanism will support fundamental research into atomic-scale physics and inform future work in molecular electronics, spintronics, and microfluidic technologies.

The research in Augsburg received funding through the German Research Foundation (DFG) Collaborative Research Center 1585, alongside supercomputing project funding for LiCCA. Additional institutional support was granted by the European Research Council under Horizon 2020, the Israel Science Foundation, the Minerva Foundation, the Villum Foundation, the Novo Nordisk Foundation, the Independent Research Fund Denmark, the National Key Research and Development Program of China, and the National Natural Science Foundation of China.

Questions people ask

How does the molecular Archimedes screw generate rotation?

Electrons passing through the helical molecule transfer angular momentum to its structure. The direction of rotation is determined by the handedness of the molecule's coil and the polarity of the applied electrical voltage.

Why does the molecule conduct more electricity when spinning?

Quantum mechanical simulations conducted on the LiCCA supercomputer show that stationary molecules avoid certain high-conduction orientations. Continuous rotation forces the molecule through these previously inaccessible orientations, boosting conductivity up to 100-fold.

Can the rotation speed or threshold be mechanically controlled?

Yes. Researchers adjusted the distance between the two gold electrode tips, stretching or compressing the molecule to change the voltage threshold required to trigger the sharp current surge.

Is this molecular motor ready for commercial technology?

No. The system is a laboratory prototype tested near absolute zero, though researchers state it demonstrates principles that could inform molecular electronics, spintronics, and microfluidics.
